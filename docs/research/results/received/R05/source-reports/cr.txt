## Data Sources and Evidence Quality  
- **Source Types:** In a real project, data could come from *primary sources* (surveys, experiments, interviews) or *secondary sources* (published papers, datasets, reports). ## Methodologies Used and Their Limitations  
- **Common Approaches:** Typical research methods fall into broad categories:
  - **Qualitative methods** (e.g. interviews, focus groups, case studies): These yield in-depth insights into human behaviors or perceptions. Strengths include rich, contextual understanding; limitations include small sample sizes and reduced generalizability. As one analyst notes, findings from *non-representative samples may not apply outside that sample*. Qualitative results are often hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive.  
  - **Quantitative methods** (e.g. surveys, experiments, statistical analysis): These yield numeric data from larger samples. They can test hypotheses and estimate effect sizes. However, they may “miss the *why*” behind patterns (i.e. they often identify correlations without explaining motivations). Quantitative studies can suffer from measurement error and biases if questionnaires or instruments are flawed. Even *gold-standard* randomized trials have limits: they are “expensive, time-consuming, and often impractical” for many questions, and results may lack external validity.  
  - **Mixed methods:** Combining qualitative and quantitative approaches can offset individual weaknesses. For example, a survey (quant) might be followed by interviews (qual) to interpret unexpected results. Mixed methods require more resources and complex design, but can provide a fuller picture.  
  - **Secondary research (literature reviews, meta-analyses):** These synthesize existing studies. Systematic reviews follow strict protocols; meta-analyses pool numerical results from multiple studies to get stronger inferences. Strength: high-level overview of the field. Limitation: they depend on the quality of the original studies and can inherit their biases or publication bias (positive results are more likely to be published).  
  - **Modeling and Simulation:** In technical fields, mathematical or computational models predict outcomes. Strength: can test scenarios in silico; limitations: results depend heavily on model assumptions and parameters, which may oversimplify reality.  
- **Limitations and Biases:** Every method has caveats. As one guide emphasizes, *“no single study is free of methodological limitations”*. Key issues include: **sampling bias** (who was studied?), **measurement error** (how accurate are our instruments?); **confounding variables** (unobserved factors that may influence outcomes); **study design constraints** (e.g. cross-sectional designs cannot establish causation); and **small sample sizes** (leading to low statistical power). Researchers must acknowledge these in interpreting results.  
- **Comparison Table:** Below is a summary of common research methodologies with their typical strengths and weaknesses:

| Methodology            | Description                                      | Strengths                                         | Limitations                                         |
|------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| **Qualitative** (e.g. interviews, case studies) | Exploratory data (words, observations) from small groups or documents. | Rich, detailed insights; flexible to explore new themes. | Small samples limit generalizability; findings may be subjective or hard to replicate. |
| **Quantitative** (e.g. surveys, experiments) | Numerical data from larger samples or experiments.      | Can generalize to populations; strong statistical validity. | May miss context or “why”; risk of measurement bias; correlation ≠ causation. |
| **Mixed Methods**      | Combination of qualitative and quantitative.    | Balances depth and breadth; cross-validates findings.  | Resource-intensive; complex analysis; requires multidisciplinary expertise. |
| **Literature Review / Meta-analysis** | Systematic compilation of existing studies.               | Provides comprehensive overview; increases evidence strength (meta-analysis). | Dependent on existing literature quality; prone to publication bias (positive-result bias). |
| **Modeling/Simulation** | Computational or mathematical modeling of processes. | Allows testing hypothetical scenarios; cost-effective. | Outcomes rely on model assumptions; may oversimplify real systems. |

- **Suitability:** The choice of method depends on the question. In practice, constraints (time, budget, data availability) heavily influence methodology choice, so acknowledging limitations is critical.
