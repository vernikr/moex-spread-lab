# Channel Briefs and Accessibility

- **MOEX Press Center (https://www.moex.com/ru/news/):** Official news and press releases (in Russian and English). Each item has a unique ID and timestamp (e.g. `n104414` for a 21.09.2026 update). News articles are static HTML (archive accessible via “Поиск по новостям” or RSS). There is no edit history (once published it stands). Transcripts are the text itself.  
  - *Export plan:* Scrape news HTML or RSS for relevant categories (e.g. “Срочный рынок”, “Risk parameters”). Example: [31] shows risk-limit changes. Use a scheduled fetch or RSS pull each day; all items are timestamped. No signed-in access needed.  
  - *Notes:* All content is official press text. Older news remain accessible. No login or paywall. 

- **Bank of Russia website (cbr.ru):** Contains press releases and event announcements. For example, the BoR’s English site listed a 20 Oct 2026 press-conference notice. There are categories for “News” and “Decisions”. Russian version has more content (e.g. digital ruble start). Each item shows date/time and often downloadable PDF.  
  - *Export plan:* Monitor the “Новости” section or RSS (if available), and specific event URLs (pattern `press/event?id=XXXX`). The English site (cbr.ru/eng/) offers some content but often lags the Russian. Save pages (or PDFs via “Save as PDF” links) for transcripts. Example excerpt: digital ruble news.  
  - *Channel:* BoR’s official social media (VKontakte *vk.com*) is noted (broadcast press events), but it’s read-only via cbr.ru. Historical posts are not easily fetched without VK API. So focus on website text.

- **EU Council press (consilium.europa.eu):** Authoritative statements on EU-Russia relations. The press release of 12 Oct 2026 is an example (in English). These are static pages with time stamps.  
  - *Export plan:* Subscribe to EU press releases or scrape their news pages (by date) for “Russia” and “Ukraine”. The Council site provides RSS and API. Each release is dated and signed (no edits). Transcripts = the HTML text.  
  - *Justification:* These releases signal major geopolitical events (sanctions, agreements). Example: listing of 1569 new sanctioned entities, with detailed text. Although not domestic, this info would have cross-border market impact.

- **U.S. Treasury / OFAC press (home.treasury.gov):** Lists new sanctions actions on Russia (e.g. EO 14024). For example, a Treasury release in Feb 2022 detailed prohibitions on Russian Central Bank bond dealings. Each action is dated and archived.  
  - *Export plan:* Pull “Recent Actions” JSON feed or scrape search results for “Russia” on OFAC. Releases have names/IDs and HTML content. Example excerpt: CBR sanctions listing.  
  - *Notes:* These are factual lists (names, dates). Usefulness: they mark when major financial restrictions took effect. 

- **(State media like TASS/RIA):** *Optional.* These agencies publish day-to-day news. No formal archival issues, but filtering is needed (many news items not trade-relevant). We did not find publicly citable examples beyond general commentary. If used, note: **potential conflict** (state narrative bias).  

*Where deeper export isn’t needed:* We do not plan exhaustive historical scrapes of Telegram or YouTube. Similarly, BoR VK and other social media are not ideal for automated full-text extraction over history. We focus on official web corpora and releases.

# Lexical and Event Features

Based on the above sources, possible features include:

- **News-sentiment indices:** Construct sentiment measures from official news text. For example, an “economic news sentiment” index (ENS) built from counts of positive/negative words is highly predictive of macro turning points.  The ECB found a newspaper-based ENS and its square gave an ROC AUC of 0.93 for predicting recessions, far above chance.  Analogously, one could track sentiment in Russian news (e.g. “рост” vs “снижение”, “кризис” vs “рост” etc).  Even keyword counts (like an R-word index – occurrences of “кризис” or “рецессия”) have proven useful.  

- **Event flags from text:** Extract deterministic events in free text. E.g., scanning MOEX news for words like “изменение риск-параметров” (as in [31]) or “аукцион состоится” yields new event indicators (e.g. margin changes or liquidity injections). Similarly, BoR text might announce “продажи валюты” or “интервенции”, which are dated events. Unlike scheduled corporate actions, these often arrive asynchronously via news, so can provide a signal beyond pre-known calendars.

- **Lexical tokens for policy content:** Key phrases in central bank or government releases. For example, presence of terms like “инфляции”, “ставка”, “рецессия”, “санкции” (in BoR or other speeches) can flag policy stances or external shocks. The BoR’s announcement of the new digital ruble scheme or Governor’s statements could be parsed for cues. 

- **External shock indicators:** Derived from foreign announcements. For example, incorporate a feature indicating “EU sanctions expanded today” (true/false) from [33], or “US sanctions on Russian banking” from [64]. These are binary event flags that baseline price models wouldn’t know in advance.

Each candidate text feature should be tested against the price‐only and dated‐event baselines. For example, a simple baseline is “days since last announced policy change” or “no. of articles with keyword” to see if the text adds incremental predictive power. The ECB study suggests indices like ENS can beat traditional indicators (like PMI or yield spread). We would similarly compare any Russian-news sentiment index against a baseline that has only market prices and formal calendar events (dividends, auctions, etc.).

# Ablation Testing Protocol

We will simulate chronological (walk-forward) model evaluation. Each text feature will be trained/fitted only on data up to the decision time, with no lookahead. We compare models with and without each feature (ablation). Key points:

- **Time resolution:** News should be timestamped; we assume fetching delay 3–90 minutes. In simulation, a signal from an 11:00 news is only used for trades after that time.  

- **Feature pipelining:** All text (and dictionary) development occurs in the past. E.g., a sentiment lexicon or keyword list is fixed before backtesting. We do not use any future data in constructing text features.

- **Benchmarks:** Baseline models use (1) price-derived features (e.g. momentum, volatility) and (2) simple event flags (like auction calendars, known policy meetings). Each text-augmented model is tested against these. The reported metric is excess P&L over the matched (simulated) LQDT counterfactual.

- **Cost/latency assessment:** Some features (like counting words) are cheap (few CPU cycles) and can be computed quickly. More complex processing (e.g. LLM embeddings via Gemini) is costly and subject to quota. The protocol will note which signals require substantial computation or risk LLM unreliability. For example, deterministic keyword counts have negligible cost; using a language model to parse nuance would be much slower and possibly unpredictable.  

- **Rejection criteria:** If a feature cannot be sourced timely (e.g. if a news channel isn’t reliably archived, or is often edited without record), it may be dropped. Any feature that consistently fails to beat the lexicon/PCA baseline (constructed using identical data splits and costs) will be abandoned. 

- **Bias check:** We must ensure features do not rely on future-curated data. For example, an index based on a later-corrected news article is disallowed. The ECB case used contemporaneous print media, analogous to our approach. We will log source URLs and their publish timestamps to avoid retrospective bias.

For unresolved methodological questions (e.g. best sentiment lexicons in finance, LLM techniques), we plan targeted literature retrieval: 

- **Sentiment dictionaries:** Look up Loughran & McDonald’s financial sentiment lexicons (J. Fin. 2011) for domain-specific word lists. Also search for Russian-language sentiment resources (e.g. in academic NLP corpora).  
- **News analytics surveys:** Find reviews or books on “news sentiment and asset returns”. For instance, Tetlock (2007) and more recent overviews (Clement et al.). Possibly “Handbook of News Analytics” if available.  
- **Time-series NLP:** Seek texts on online learning with text features and accounting for multiple time zones. For example, find chapters in econometrics/NLP textbooks covering high-frequency news data.  
- **Policy-target scripts:** For event flag extraction, review any standard coding of government announcements (e.g. NIPA, statistical bulletins). 

We will use public search (Google Scholar, institutional sites) or university library access for PDFs. We will note which sections to consult (e.g. Lexicon creation chapters, regression tables) when relevant. No paid databases or private codes will be used.

# Conclusions and Assumptions

Based on available evidence, the most promising text features are derived from **authoritative announcements**: exchange press releases, central bank communications, and official sanction declarations. These contain timely, structured information (e.g. policy changes, new regulations, financial restrictions) that price models and dated-event flags alone miss. For instance, MOEX’s own newswire and BoR releases are fact-based texts tied to specific dates. They can be scraped with minimal latency and used to create real-time signals (e.g. a sudden margin easing or a new ruble policy). 

The ECB study shows that even newspaper‐based sentiment indices can be highly predictive. Translated to our context, simple sentiment or keyword indices from Russian finance news could capture shifts in market mood or risk. However, any learned (ML) model on text would need careful cross-validation to avoid overfitting or leaking knowledge (ensuring only past text is used).

**Material conflicts/unknowns:** All Russian official sources are under state influence, but they are primary facts (dates, figures) and thus valuable. External sources (EU/US) clearly have geopolitical bias, so any feature from them is a **signal of international pressure** (which is indeed useful). We mark those as such. We assume that posting dates on official sites reflect true release time; any delays or revisions are risks. We will log first-available times (via web archives if needed). 

**Falsification criteria:** A text-derived feature fails if adding it to a model does not improve (or degrades) excess return over the baseline under any tested train-test splits. Also, if acquiring the text requires paid or illicit means (e.g. private Telegram channels), we discard it. Finally, if a feature’s source is altered/hidden historically (e.g. a news page is updated without record), we cannot use it retrospectively.

This plan guides a systematic evaluation: first attempt simple lexical/event features (word counts, sentiment scores) from the identified official feeds; if promising, consider more complex NLP under the cost/latency budget. All analyses will be documented with source citations and versioned timestamps of news content.
