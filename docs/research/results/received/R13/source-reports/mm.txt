# Title
Regime Conditioning for MOEX Futures Spreads: When Simple State Splits Add Value and When They Just Add Complexity

## 1. Direct Answer and Framing

Observable market-wide or instrument-family regime information can improve out‑of‑sample net decisions versus a no‑regime baseline, but only under tightly controlled, **very simple** regimes and **pre-specified points of impact** (primarily entry gating and position sizing). As regime models become more complex (hidden Markov chains, many states, rich feature sets), the incremental benefit over robust fixed-parameter baselines tends to evaporate out-of-sample and is quickly dominated by overfitting risk and implementation cost.

For a MOEX futures-spread pilot constrained to RUB 30,000 capital, walk‑forward validation, and LQDT as the cash benchmark, the most defensible path is:

- First, define and test **three transparent, decision-time-observable regime splits**:
  - Market volatility regime via Russian Volatility Index (RVI) and/or realized volatility.
  - Instrument liquidity regime via spread/volume/open interest.
  - Carry/term-structure regime via observable roll yield/contango vs backwardation.
- Second, **limit regime impact** to a small, predeclared set of levers (e.g., entry on/off and size cap) and test them against a fixed-parameter baseline with identical cost/delay assumptions.
- Third, **only if** at least one simple split survives falsification and ablation on MOEX data should you consider more elaborate hidden‑state or change‑point models as optional, separate hypotheses.

The single most actionable takeaway is: **design R13 as an incremental “regime filter” experiment on top of 1–2 simple MOEX spread baselines; treat HMM/Markov-switching and multi‑indicator composites as phase‑2 research that must justify its own complexity via clearly pre-registered, out‑of‑sample gains in RUB excess over LQDT.**

## 2. Baseline and Validation Skeleton (Anchoring the Comparison)

**The baseline must be a simple, fully specified spread rule, fit and tested under the same walk‑forward skeleton as any regime-conditioned variant.** Without this, it is impossible to isolate regime value from generic model-tuning gains.

### 2.1 Candidate MOEX Baseline Families

MOEX natively lists index and sector futures (IMOEXF, MIX, MXI, OGI, sector indices) with clear contract sizes, margin rates and tick values, including a mini MOEX Russia Index futures (MXI) with margin around a few thousand RUB per contract, suitable for a RUB 30,000 pilot.[E50][E51][E52][E53] Calendar spread instruments exist and are traded as unified codes (e.g., OGI-6.26-9.26), with synthetic matching and archives of spread prices, volume and trades.[E17][E60][E62][E43]

Given these constraints, two baselines are practically defensible:

1. **Index calendar-carry baseline**  
   - Universe: MXI or another small index future where one calendar spread consumes <50–60% of pilot capital.  
   - Trade object: exchange-listed calendar spreads (e.g., MXI-9.24-12.24).[E60]  
   - Logic: fixed rule to buy contango (or backwardation) mean-reversion or a simple “roll carry” rule (e.g., long near/short far when curve steepness exceeds historical percentile).  
   - Holding: fixed horizon (e.g., 10–20 trading days) or exit on spread reversion to mean ±kσ, with no regime info.

2. **Sector index vs MOEX Russia Index mean‑reversion baseline**  
   - Universe: OGI (Oil & Gas Index futures) vs MXI (MOEX Russia Index mini), both with tick values denominated in RUB and available calendar spreads.[E39][E49][E51][E54]  
   - Trade object: spread of front-month OGI vs MXI; simple cointegration/z‑score reversion rule with symmetric entry/exit bands, fixed holding max.

These baselines are fully “no regime”: all parameters are estimated on training windows only; the same walk‑forward is used later for regime‑conditioned variants.

### 2.2 Validation Skeleton and Data Availability

MOEX provides:

- Daily aggregated futures results and archive CSVs for spread instruments from at least the mid‑2000s; demo futures data from 2014–2017 are explicitly free.[E31][E32][E34][E36][E38]  
- Free ISS API access with 15‑minute delayed data across markets, including candles, contract specs, and spread instruments; real‑time or depth may require subscription.[E33][E44][E45][E46][E61]

This enables a **chronological walk‑forward** design:

- Training windows (e.g., 1–2 years) for baseline parameter selection.  
- Forward test windows (e.g., 3–6 months) aligned so that:
  - No position opened in the test window depends on parameters or state estimates calibrated on later data.  
  - Gaps around training/test splits account for maximum holding time and 3–90 minute signal‑to‑execution delays.  
- A final untouched holdout window (e.g., latest 12 months of continuous data) reserved until all hypotheses are frozen.[E65][E69]

LQDT is a MOEX-listed money-market ETF investing in short-term repo and cash instruments; it is explicitly marketed as a liquidity and cash-management fund with low credit and market risk.[E19][E23][E35] This makes its daily NAV series a suitable cash benchmark for RUB capital. Excess performance is therefore measured as **cumulative RUB P&L net of all fees/slippage minus the value of the same cash left entirely in LQDT** (per the embedded assumptions).

## 3. What “Regime” Means Here: Observable vs Hidden

### 3.1 No MOEX-Specific Regime Models, but Rich Generic Literature

Academic and practitioner literature on regime-switching models is extensive for spreads and volatility, especially in equities, FX, and oil:

- Hidden Markov and Markov-switching models are applied to statistical arbitrage and pairs trading by explicitly modeling spreads as mean-reverting processes with discrete states.[E2][E7][E9][E11]  
- Regime-switching and HAR‑RV models improve volatility forecasts for oil futures versus single‑regime models.[E8][E9]  
- Brent–WTI spread studies employ Markov regime-switching to capture structural regimes in integration and pricing; some report out-of-sample improvements.[E14][E15][E11]  
- Energy futures spread trading (Kanamura et al.) finds high‑volatility regimes coincide with the most profitable trades, but only in-sample results are given.[E27]

Searches in both English and Russian did not uncover any academic paper applying regime-switching specifically to MOEX futures spreads.[E16][E17][E18][E19][E21][E22] MOEX documentation on spreads and calendar spreads explains mechanics and fee/margin treatment, but does not embed regime or state modeling.[E20][E27][E33]

Conclusion: **methods are portable, but no “ready‑made” MOEX regime model exists**; this project must adapt generic techniques and rigorously test them on MOEX data.

### 3.2 Observable Regimes: Volatility, Liquidity, Carry

Given the pilot’s constraints, the first line of attack is **observable, rule-based regime splits** that use only information available at decision time:

1. **Volatility regime**  
   - Market-wide: RVI, the Russian Volatility Index, is MOEX’s implied volatility benchmark, calculated from RTS index options to measure 30‑day expected volatility; it is published every 15 seconds and has a historical archive.[E4][E36][E37][E59][E66][E70]  
   - Instrument-level: rolling realized volatility of the spread or underlying contracts, computed using past daily (or intraday, if available) returns.  
   - Literature typically defines low/normal/high (and sometimes crisis) regimes by quantiles of realized or implied volatility.[E10][E11][E31][E32]  

2. **Liquidity regime**  
   - Futures liquidity is characterized by depth, bid‑ask spread, and volume/open interest.[E22][E24][E25][E26]  
   - An OFR working paper and related research show three latent liquidity regimes (high, medium, low price-impact) are sufficient to describe futures market liquidity, modeled via HMMs with price‑impact measures.[E25][E33][E35]  
   - For MOEX, daily spread, volume and open interest are observable via ISS and official reports.[E23][E26][E29][E38]

3. **Carry / term-structure regime**  
   - In futures, “carry” is the return if the underlying price stays unchanged; roll yield is the difference between futures P&L and spot price change.[E15][E41]  
   - Contango/backwardation are distinct term-structure regimes: contango when futures exceed spot/expected spot; backwardation when they are below.[E24][E25]  
   - Commodity and energy research distinguishes carry regimes via term-structure slopes and S‑U (stock‑to‑use) ratios, with some evidence of regime-dependent hedge ratios or carry premia, but without a standardized HMM-like “carry regime” modeling framework.[E16][E38][E39][E40]

These observable regimes are naturally **aligned with decision time** and do not require smoothing over the full sample; regime membership can be computed using trailing windows only.

### 3.3 Hidden-State and Change-Point Models

The classical hidden-state toolkit includes:

- Hamilton’s Markov switching regression framework,[E63][E67] hidden Markov models and Hidden Semi‑Markov Models (HSMM) for volatility, liquidity and spread dynamics.[E1][E12][E19][E25][E26]  
- PELT change-point detection, a pruned exact linear time algorithm for detecting multiple change points in time series by minimizing a penalized cost function; empirical studies show it is effective for financial time-series segmentation.[E26][E27][E31][E64][E68]

Evidence on out-of-sample value is mixed:

- One SSRN study on volatility-regime HSMMs reports that while HSMM outperforms HMM in-sample, the advantage largely disappears out‑of‑sample, underscoring overfitting risk when using flexible regime architectures.[E17]  
- Risk and VaR practitioner notes emphasize that for VaR and similar forecasting tasks, parsimonious models routinely beat elaborate regime-switching models; more regimes always increase in‑sample fit but often degrade out-of-sample performance and stability.[E12][E15][E6][E30]  

Hence, **hidden‑state models belong as second‑stage hypotheses**: only to be tried if simple observable state splits show robust incremental value and if the project is willing to invest in significantly higher modeling complexity.

## 4. Where Regime Information Should Act in the Strategy

To avoid conflating many changes with “regime effect,” you must pre‑declare **where** regime information is allowed to modify behaviour.

Given pilot capital and manual execution lags, four levers are plausible; they should not all be turned on in the same variant:

1. **Entry gating (filter)**  
   - Strategy trades only when the current regime is deemed “favourable” (e.g., low volatility, or high liquidity & neutral carry).  
   - This directly addresses execution risk and tail moves: for example, literature shows trend or spread strategies may be particularly sensitive to volatility regimes.[E36][E32]  
   - This is the cleanest place to start: same signal logic, but suppressed in disallowed regimes.

2. **Position sizing / notional cap**  
   - Maintain a base unit size (e.g., 1 spread contract) but cut size under adverse regimes (e.g., halve or set to zero when volatility RVI above crisis threshold).  
   - The OFR liquidity-regime work implies trading costs differ across hidden regimes; a simple proxy is to size down under high observed spreads/low volumes, even without HMMs.[E25][E33]

3. **Exit rules and maximum duration**  
   - Allow earlier exits or tighter stop/exit thresholds in high‑volatility or low‑liquidity regimes to mitigate gap risk.  
   - This complicates attribution more than entry gating, because trade lifecycle now depends on regime sequence, not just initial state.

4. **Instrument or spread eligibility**  
   - Permit specific spread types only in certain regimes (e.g., only trade long‑carry calendar spreads when the curve is in strong backwardation and volatility is low).  
   - This is effectively a conditional universe filter based on carry regime.

For R13, **priority should be: entry gating and size cap**. Exit and eligibility changes introduce interactions that are harder to attribute cleanly in small samples.

## 5. Simple Observable Regime Designs for R13

This section translates the conceptual regimes into concrete **hypothesis cards** that satisfy the project’s constraints.

### 5.1 Volatility Regime via RVI and Realized Volatility

MOEX’s RVI measures 30‑day implied volatility from RTS index options; the methodology was updated effective 23 March 2026, with data published every 15 seconds and historical archives available.[E36][E37][E59][E66][E70] For MOEX index or sector spreads, RVI provides a liquid, market‑wide volatility proxy.

**Hypothesis Card V1: RVI‑filtered entry**

- **Baseline**: index calendar spread mean‑reversion on MXI or sector vs MXI, with fixed entry/exit rules.  
- **Regime definition (decision-time info)**:
  - Compute rolling percentiles of daily RVI over the last 252 trading days (approximately one year).  
  - Label:  
    - Low vol: RVI below 40th percentile.  
    - Normal: between 40th and 80th percentile.  
    - High/crisis: above 80th percentile.
- **Regime action**:  
  - Allow new entries only when RVI is in Low or Normal regimes.  
  - Suppress all entries when RVI is High/crisis. Existing trades follow baseline exit logic.  
- **Rationale**: volatility regimes are much easier to forecast than direction and strongly affect spread behaviour and execution risk.[E36][E32] A simple volatility gate can reduce tail losses without complex hidden-state modeling.  
- **Leakage control**:  
  - Use only past RVI values to determine percentiles and current state; no smoothing with future values, no recursive revision.  
  - Recompute regime boundaries only at walk‑forward re‑estimation dates, not continuously.

**Hypothesis Card V2: Spread realized volatility gate**

Where RVI may be less representative (e.g., OGI vs MXI spread), substitute a **spread-level realized volatility** regime:

- Realized volatility: standard deviation of daily spread returns over trailing 20 days.  
- Thresholds: median and 80th percentile of trailing 200‑day vol.  
- Entry gating: as in V1 (no new trades under “high-vol” regime).

This remains fully observable and avoids relying on options markets.

### 5.2 Liquidity Regime via Bid–Ask Spread, Volume, Open Interest

The IMF and OFR studies conceptualize **liquidity regimes** as high/medium/low price-impact states, reflected in spreads, depth and volume.[E26][E33][E35][E36] MOEX publishes daily volume, number of trades and open interest for futures and spreads; bid‑ask data are in daily spread archives but live order-book depth requires subscription.[E23][E26][E29][E60][E62]

**Hypothesis Card L1: Liquidity filter based on quoted spread and volume**

- **Metrics** (per instrument or spread):  
  - End-of-day bid–ask spread as fraction of mid (from MOEX spread archive).[E60][E62]  
  - Daily volume (contracts) in the same spread.[E34][E38]  
- **Regime definition** (trailing 120 trading days window):  
  - High-liquidity: spread below 40th percentile and volume above 60th percentile.  
  - Low-liquidity: spread above 80th percentile or volume below 20th percentile.  
  - Mid: everything else.
- **Regime action**:  
  - Only open trades if current day is High or Mid liquidity; in Low liquidity no new trades.  
  - Optional variant: cap position size to 1 contract regardless of pilot scale in Low liquidity but still allow entries (ablation, see §7).
- **Rationale**: OFR research demonstrates that three liquidity regimes suffice to describe futures market microstructure, and trading costs differ significantly across them.[E25][E33][E35] For a small pilot with tight drawdown budgets, avoiding “thin” days is a straightforward risk reducer.  
- **Execution realism**: since trades in the pilot will be executed with 3–90 minute delays, this regime is evaluated at **previous day’s close** to determine whether next‑day signals are eligible.

### 5.3 Carry / Term-Structure Regime

Commodity spread research distinguishes **carry regimes** by term-structure shape (contango vs backwardation) and underlying storage/carry cost ratios.[E14][E24][E25][E4] Futures carry is the expected return if spot stays unchanged; roll yield equals futures P&L minus spot change.[E15][E41]

For MOEX index and sector futures, the economics differ from physical commodities but calendar spreads still encode expectations about roll yield and dividends. For OGI vs MXI, oil-related components may embed sector-specific premia.

**Hypothesis Card C1: Calendar-carry filter for spread trades**

- **Metric**: slope of term structure for the underlying index or sector, using near and next contract mid-prices (or the listed calendar spread price on MOEX).[E2][E41][E43]  
  - Define normalized carry: \( \text{carry}_{t} = \frac{F_{2,t} - F_{1,t}}{T_2 - T_1} / F_{1,t} \), where \(F_{1}, F_{2}\) are near and next futures, \(T_2 - T_1\) time to maturity difference (years).  
- **Regime definition** (again via trailing quantiles):  
  - Strong backwardation: carry below 20th percentile.  
  - Neutral: between 20th and 80th percentiles.  
  - Strong contango: above 80th percentile.
- **Regime action** (one example consistent with cost-of-carry reasoning):  
  - Only allow **mean-reversion calendar spreads betting on flattening** when term-structure is in strong contango (high roll cost, potential for normalization).  
  - Disallow such trades in strong backwardation, where spreads may reflect scarcity or risk premia.  
- **Rationale**: empirical commodity work shows that spread behaviour and hedge ratios differ across carry-cost regimes; ignoring this can mis-specify risk.[E16][E38][E39] Even if out-of-sample performance is ambiguous, carry state is mechanistically tied to expected roll yield.

This card is more economically nuanced than V or L but still uses only contemporaneous futures prices. It is best applied to strategies that explicitly exploit calendar term structure, less to index vs sector spreads.

## 6. Hidden-State and Change-Point Hypotheses (Optional, Second Stage)

If and only if at least one observable regime filter (V1, L1, C1) survives falsification (§7), the project may consider limited hidden-state models.

### 6.1 Hidden Markov Model on Spread Dynamics

Several studies demonstrate HMM-based spread models for statistical arbitrage and pairs trading in equities and energy markets.[E2][E3][E7][E9][E36] Typical setup:

- Observed variable: spread \(S_t\) and/or its increments.  
- Hidden state: discrete regime \(Z_t \in \{1,2\}\) indicating “mean-reverting” vs “non‑reverting” or “high‑vol” vs “low‑vol”.  
- Dynamics: \(S_t\) follows AR(1) with regime‑dependent mean and variance.

**Hypothesis Card H1: HMM mean‑reversion gating**

- **Baseline**: same as in §2, mean-reversion spread with fixed parameters.  
- **HMM estimation**:  
  - Fit a 2‑state Gaussian HMM to spread changes on the training window only, using EM algorithm.  
  - Interpret State 1 as “favourable” if it has stronger mean reversion and/or lower variance; State 2 as “unfavourable.”  
- **Regime action**:  
  - Only allow new entries when the filtered probability \(P(Z_t=1|\text{history})\) exceeds a threshold (e.g., 0.7).  
  - Otherwise, do not open trades; existing trades ignore HMM and use baseline exit.
- **Known risks**:  
  - HSMM vs HMM comparisons show in-sample advantages can vanish out‑of‑sample.[E17]  
  - Regime identification is unstable, prone to label switching and local likelihood maxima.[E12][E15][E30]  

To meet the project’s overfitting constraints, H1 should be limited to 2 states, univariate spread input, and at most one gating threshold per strategy.

### 6.2 PELT Change-Point Segmentation

The PELT algorithm provides an efficient way to detect multiple change points in the first or second moments of financial time series; empirical work shows accurate detection with linear complexity.[E26][E27][E31][E64][E68]

**Hypothesis Card P1: Regime blocks via PELT for parameter re‑estimation**

- **Objective**: not to time trades directly, but to **segment history** into stationary blocks for parameter estimation.  
- **Mechanism**:  
  - On the training window only, run PELT on the spread or volatility series to identify breakpoints.  
  - For each block, estimate separate mean-reversion parameters; then in walk‑forward, use the parameters from the most recent completed block.  
- **Action**:  
  - Entry/exit rules remain unchanged; only the estimated parameters (means, variances, thresholds) differ by epoch.  
- **Risk**: block boundaries can be sensitive to penalty choices; mis‑tuned penalties reintroduce overfitting under another guise.

Given limited payoff versus complexity, P1 is best left as an optional design extension if simple filters show strongly regime-dependent parameters.

## 7. Controlling Degrees of Freedom, Overfitting and Leakage

The core risk for all regime conditioning is that **you discover regimes because you overfit** rather than because they actually carry predictive information. Literature on regime models stresses that adding regimes always improves in‑sample fit but often degrades out-of-sample performance, especially when regimes are estimated with short crisis samples.[E12][E15][E17][E30]

### 7.1 Search Space and Hypothesis Management

To keep the search space finite and auditable:

- **Cap primary hypotheses** to at most:
  - 2 baseline strategies (Index calendar; sector vs index mean‑reversion).  
  - 3 observable regime filters (V1, L1, C1).  
  - 1–2 hidden-state variants (H1, optionally P1) only if justified.  
- For each baseline × regime combination, predefine at most **two variants** (e.g., strict vs loose threshold) and document them in a **hypothesis registry** with:
  - Name, baseline reference.  
  - Exact variable definitions and computation windows.  
  - Explicit mapping of regime to trading decisions (entry yes/no, size multiplier).  
  - The specific splits (training windows, test windows, holdout).  
  - The cost scenario (base/conservative/stress) applied.

Every unsuccessful variant must be logged rather than discarded silently; otherwise, you cannot assess how many degrees of freedom you spent to obtain a “winner.”

### 7.2 Leakage and Label Smoothing Checks

Two classes of leakage must be prevented:

1. **Forward-looking regime labels**  
   - Volatility and liquidity metrics must be computed strictly on **past data** (e.g., realized volatility over t–20…t–1).  
   - RVI regime percentiles are computed at walk‑forward re‑estimation dates only; you cannot recalibrate thresholds using information from future crises.  
   - Hidden states in HMM/HSMM are estimated using EM on training data; test-time uses only filtered probabilities without re-estimating using test data.

2. **Market closure and contract-change events**  
   - MOEX has specific annual non-business days and holiday schedules; for 2026, futures trading is modified or closed on several days in January, March and May.[E20][E34][E38]  
   - Policy-driven contract changes (e.g., halt of Urals oil futures after 2012)[E38] and major sanctions/regulatory shocks must be encoded as **exogenous events, not learnable regimes**.  
   - If major structural events coincide with certain regimes (e.g., high RVI during sanctions), you must not claim causal predictive power from the regime label itself.

The implementation should include refutation tests where regime labels are deliberately lagged further (e.g., using one-week-old values) to ensure apparent performance is not just reactive.

### 7.3 Ablation Protocol

For each promising regime‑conditioned strategy:

1. **Baseline vs filtered comparison**  
   - Same signal logic and parameters; difference is only regime gating (entries suppressed in certain states).  
   - Compare: number of trades, average P&L per trade, drawdown, and net RUB excess over LQDT under identical cost scenarios.

2. **No‑action ablation**  
   - Instead of suppressing entries, tag each trade with the regime it would have been initiated in (but always execute it).  
   - Evaluate if P&L conditional on regime differs significantly (e.g., trades initiated in high RVI produce much worse outcomes).  
   - If conditional performance is indistinguishable, using regime for gating adds complexity without information.

3. **Randomized regime gate baseline**  
   - Randomly assign the same fraction of days to “blocked” as the real regime does, independent of true state.  
   - If the real regime gate does not materially outperform this random gate, its information content is suspect.

4. **Cross-regime robustness**  
   - Test whether parameters tuned in one dominant regime (e.g., low volatility years) still work reasonably in others. Excess sensitivity suggests overfitting.

## 8. Cost, Execution and Resource Considerations

### 8.1 Fees, Margin, Liquidity and Position Limits

MOEX publishes derivatives fee schedules and margin requirements; exchange fees for commodity contracts are on the order of a few basis points per notional and clearing fees similar in magnitude, although tables must be checked for current validity as many documents are dated up to April 2024.[E18][E21][E22][E23] Futures margin requirements vary across contracts, usually a single‑digit to low‑double‑digit percent of notional.[E3][E11][E53][E58] The MXI mini futures’ first-level initial margin is in the low thousands of RUB per contract, making a one‑spread position well within a RUB 30,000 test budget with room for variation margin and fees.[E53]

Concentration and position limits exist but are defined in notional or contracts; for large index futures such as MIX, limits are thousands of RUB per contract at first concentration level.[E38][E39] With pilot-sized exposure (0–2 contracts per spread), such limits are effectively non‑binding; regime research can ignore them except as part of structural risk controls.

Liquidity-wise, MOEX stresses high-liquidity derivatives with tight spreads for major contracts; official analytics show BR, Si, RI, GD as focus contracts with published open interest statistics.[E2][E27][E29][E38] However, regime design must treat low-liquidity tails carefully via the L1 filter, especially for sector calendars.

### 8.2 Data and Computational Resources

Data:

- End-of-day futures and spread series from MOEX archives: sufficient for daily‑bar strategies and initial regime exploration.[E31][E32][E34][E36][E38]  
- ISS API for 15‑minute delayed candles and current spread quotes: aligns well with the 3–90 minute manual delay constraint.[E33][E44][E46][E61]  
- RVI time series: MOEX provides historical RVI index data and futures references.[E36][E37][E59][E66][E70]  
- LQDT NAV history: daily NAV and price data from MOEX and related sources.[E23][E34][E35][E39]

Computation:

- The pilot environment (MacBook Pro i7, 16 GB RAM; small VPS) is ample for daily‑bar walk‑forward, simple regime filters, and 1D or small‑state HMMs.  
- PELT and Markov-switching models are available in standard R/Python libraries, with feasible runtimes at daily frequency.

No paid or low‑latency data are needed for R13; ISS delayed data and historical archives suffice.

## 9. Scenario Analysis: When Regime Conditioning Helps vs Hurts

### Scenario A: Volatility-Gated Filter Adds Value

- Volatility regimes (RVI or realized) turn out to be strongly associated with trade outcomes: trades initiated in high‑vol regimes have worse risk-adjusted returns and deeper drawdowns; gating them out materially reduces worst‑case excess drawdown versus LQDT, even at the cost of fewer trades.  
- Under all cost scenarios (base, conservative, stress), the volatility-gated strategy demonstrates higher median and lower left‑tail of excess RUB performance versus the baseline.  
- In this world, **simple observable regime conditioning is worth including in production**; hidden-state models may incrementally refine volatility signals but must justify extra complexity.

### Scenario B: Liquidity Regime is Informative but Carry is Not

- Trades in low‑liquidity regimes exhibit larger slippage and more unfilled/partial fills, pushing realized P&L below theoretical P&L; the L1 liquidity filter reduces cost drag and shortfall relative to LQDT.  
- Carry regimes, however, may not display statistically distinct performance; calendar‑carry filters either cut too many good trades or fail to exclude bad ones.  
- The project would retain liquidity gating as an **execution‑risk management layer** and drop C1.

### Scenario C: No Regime Filter Shows Stable Incremental Value

- None of V1, L1 or C1 survive ablation: conditional performance differences across regimes are small or unstable; out-of-sample and holdout gains are negligible versus the no-regime baseline when costs and drawdown bounds are fully accounted for.  
- Hidden-state attempts (H1, P1) show typical overfitting patterns: good in‑sample fit, no consistent holdout gains, high sensitivity to number of states or penalty choice.  
- For a capital‑constrained, manual pilot with strict drawdown limits, **the rational choice is to defer regime conditioning**, focusing on robust fixed-parameter baselines until larger datasets or more structurally diverse episodes accumulate.

## 10. Recommendations for Designing R13

Prioritized from immediate design decisions to longer-term options:

### 10.1 Immediate (Design Decisions Before Any Coding)

1. **Fix 1–2 Baselines and Lock Them**  
   - Select one index calendar and one sector‑vs‑index mean‑reversion baseline that are feasible for MXI/OGI‑level margins and RUB 30k capital.  
   - Fully specify: signal definition, parameter estimation windows, entry/exit and maximum holding, allowed contracts, and whether trades are at market or mid‑plus‑slippage.

2. **Adopt a Single Walk-Forward Skeleton**  
   - Define training windows (e.g., 2016–2019; 2019–2022), intermediate tests, and final holdout (e.g., 2023–2025).  
   - Freeze the skeleton and reuse it for **every strategy** including future ones.

3. **Pre‑Register Regime Levers**  
   - For R13, allow regime info to affect **only**: (a) entry gating and (b) size multiplier.  
   - Exit rules and eligible instrument set remain unchanged versus the baseline to isolate the effect.

4. **Select State Variables and Windows**  
   - Volatility: RVI (market) and/or 20‑day realized volatility of the spread.  
   - Liquidity: previous day’s bid–ask spread fraction and volume.  
   - Carry: term-structure slope using near vs next futures.  
   - For each, fix trailing windows for percentile computations (e.g., 120–252 days) and do not revisit.

### 10.2 Short-Term (First Implementation and Tests)

5. **Implement and Test Observable Filters First (V1, L1, C1)**  
   - For each baseline, create variants with each filter separately (no stacking initially).  
   - Run full walk‑forward with cost scenarios aligned with project assumptions; record net RUB excess over LQDT.

6. **Perform Ablation and Random-Gate Refutation**  
   - For each filter, run the ablations outlined in §7.3 to verify genuine information content.  
   - Any filter that fails to beat its random gate or shows no conditional P&L difference by regime should be discarded.

7. **Stress with Pilot Constraints**  
   - Simulate actual account drawdowns using the embedded capital tests (RUB 5k/10k/15k underperformance triggers), ensuring that improved per‑trade metrics translate into pathwise drawdown control versus LQDT.  
   - Verify that regime filters do not inadvertently cluster remaining trades into even more volatile periods.

### 10.3 Medium-Term (Optional Complexity, If Justified)

8. **Consider a Minimal HMM Gate (H1) Only If Observable Filters Succeed**  
   - Confine HMMs to 2 states and univariate spread input; avoid multi-asset latent factor HMMs or numerous macro inputs.  
   - Require that H1 beats both the no-regime baseline and the best observable filter by a material margin in holdout under conservative cost assumptions before considering it further.

9. **Defer Rich Regime Architectures**  
   - Multi-scale Markov-Switching GARCH, cross-asset regime radars, or machine-learning based regime classifiers should be **explicitly out of scope** for R13.  
   - Only revisit if pilot moves to much larger capital, longer time horizon, and a clear need to trade through multiple structural breaks.

10. **Document Discard Criteria**  
   - A regime design should be formally retired if:
     - It fails to produce statistically and economically meaningful gains in excess RUB returns versus baseline in both main and conservative cost scenarios.  
     - It adds more than one or two parameters to the baseline without clear robustness across multiple walk‑forward segments.  
     - It relies on variables or data (e.g., order-book microstructure) that cannot be reliably sourced for the entire test horizon.

If none of the pre‑specified regime filters pass these criteria, the project should **explicitly log R13 as “no incremental evidence found; regime conditioning deferred”**, and proceed with future R‑series work based only on robust, unconditional spread rules.
